# National Savings vs Mutual Funds: Which Is Better for Pakistani Investors? (2026)

  *By Abdul Ahad · Updated 20 May 2026 · 12 min read*

  
> **Quick answer:** National Savings certificates are government-guaranteed with rates locked in at purchase but low liquidity, while SECP-regulated mutual funds offer higher potential returns and daily liquidity without a capital guarantee; many Pakistani portfolios use both.

  
  

  
  
> **The short version:** National Savings = zero risk, government-guaranteed, low liquidity — best for retirees and risk-averse investors Mutual funds = higher potential returns, daily liquidity, SECP-regulated — best for working-age investors with 3+ year horizons Best portfolios use both: CDNS for stable income, equity funds for long-term growth

  
  
This is the most common question Pakistani investors ask: should I put my money in a **National Savings certificate at CDNS**, or invest it in a **mutual fund through an AMC**? Both are legitimate, regulated options — but they serve different needs, carry different risks, and suit different types of investors.

  
This guide gives you a clear, honest comparison based on real data — current rates, actual tax treatment, and the mechanics that the brochures never explain. Whether you have PKR 5,000 or PKR 5,000,000 to invest, the framework here applies.

## How National Savings Certificates Actually Work

  
Understanding the mechanics of CDNS helps you avoid costly mistakes and choose the right certificate for your situation. Opening an account is straightforward: walk into any CDNS branch or Post Office with your original CNIC, a photocopy, and your initial investment amount in cash or a pay order. Staff will assist you with the paperwork. The entire process for a first-time investor typically takes 20–40 minutes. You receive a physical certificate or a passbook as proof of your investment, which you should keep in a secure location.

  
For those who prefer not to visit a branch, CDNS has offered the **Savings.gov.pk** online portal in recent years. You can create an account, link your bank account, and invest digitally from the comfort of your home. The portal supports the Special Savings Certificate, Regular Income Certificate, and Defence Savings Certificate. Behbood certificates — which require proof of age (60 or above) or widow status — may still require an in-person visit for the initial setup. On the portal, you can also nominate a beneficiary for any certificate, which is an important estate-planning step that many investors overlook. Note that CDNS certificates are non-transferable: unlike shares on the stock exchange, you cannot sell a certificate to another person or trade it in a secondary market.

  
Profit payment mechanics differ significantly between schemes and should influence which certificate you choose. The **Regular Income Certificate** pays monthly profits directly to your linked bank account — making it the instrument of choice for retirees who rely on investment income to meet monthly expenses. The **Special Savings Certificate** pays every six months (semi-annually), which suits investors who do not need monthly payouts and are happy to let profits accumulate between payment dates. The **Defence Savings Certificate** accumulates all profit for its full 10-year tenor and pays a lump sum at maturity — useful for disciplined long-term savers who want compounding without the temptation to spend periodic payouts.

  
Early encashment penalties follow a tiered formula published on the CDNS website. For the SSC: if you encash within the first 6 months, you receive no profit at all for that period and only the face value of your investment. Between 6 and 12 months, a lower below-market rate applies. After 12 months, a graduated profit rate kicks in that increases the longer you hold, approaching the full contracted rate as you approach the 3-year maturity. The practical rule of thumb is to treat your CDNS investment as fully committed for at least 12 months and ideally for the complete tenor. Investing money you may need in an emergency into a CDNS certificate and then encashing it early is one of the most common and costly mistakes Pakistani investors make.

  
## National Savings — Strengths and Weaknesses

  
The Central Directorate of National Savings (CDNS) has been operating since 1947. Its instruments are backed by the full faith of the Government of Pakistan — meaning the only scenario in which you lose money is a complete sovereign default, which has never occurred in Pakistan's history. This makes CDNS schemes genuinely risk-free for practical purposes.

  
    National Savings is best for:
    

      - Retirees who need predictable monthly income (Regular Income Certificates at 10.92%)

      - Senior citizens and widows (Behbood Savings Certificates offer the highest rate at 12.72%)

      - Investors with no internet access or smartphone

      - Locking in today's rates before SBP cuts further reduce yields

      - Anyone who cannot afford to lose any principal

    

  

  
**Key weakness:** Liquidity. If you encash a National Savings certificate before maturity, you lose a portion of the profit you have earned. For the Special Savings Certificate (3-year tenor), premature encashment in the first six months returns only the face value with no profit; encashment in the first year pays a reduced rate. This makes CDNS unsuitable as an emergency fund or for money you might need at short notice.

  
## Mutual Funds — Strengths and Weaknesses

  
Mutual funds offer two things National Savings cannot: **higher potential returns** and **daily liquidity**. A well-selected equity fund can deliver 20–30% annualised returns over a 5-year period, far exceeding any CDNS scheme. Even money market mutual funds — the safest mutual fund type — match or exceed current National Savings rates while allowing you to withdraw your money within 2–5 business days.

  
    Mutual Funds are best for:
    

      - Working professionals aged 25–50 who can tolerate short-term volatility

      - Investors with a 3–10 year time horizon who want to beat inflation

      - Muslim investors seeking fully Shariah-compliant investments (Islamic funds)

      - Salaried individuals wanting to automate monthly savings via SIPs (as low as PKR 1,000/month)

      - Overseas Pakistanis (via Roshan Digital Account)

    

  

  
**Key weakness:** No capital guarantee. An equity fund can lose 20–30% of its value in a bear market — and the KSE-100 has seen exactly that kind of drawdown in 2022 and other periods. Investors who panic and sell at the bottom lock in real losses. If you cannot psychologically or financially handle seeing your investment drop in value, start with a money market or income fund rather than an equity fund.

  
## The Tax Reality for Both Instruments

  
Tax treatment is one of the most misunderstood aspects of comparing these two options, and getting it wrong can cost you thousands of rupees per year. Many investors assume CDNS certificates are "tax-free" — they are not. Similarly, many avoid mutual funds thinking they carry a complex or heavy tax burden. The reality for both is nuanced but manageable.

  
For **National Savings certificates**, withholding tax (WHT) is deducted at source automatically at the time of profit payment. If you are a tax filer — meaning your name appears in the Active Tax Payers List published by the Federal Board of Revenue — WHT is **15%**. If you are a non-filer, WHT rises to **30%**. This tax is deducted before the profit reaches your bank account, so you never handle the gross amount. For filers the WHT is a final tax on this income, meaning you do not owe any additional income tax on top of it when you file your annual return.

  
For **mutual funds**, the tax picture is more nuanced. Income and money market funds that distribute dividends attract WHT of **15% for filers and 30% for non-filers** — exactly the same structure as CDNS. Equity funds that grow in NAV rather than distributing dividends are subject to Capital Gains Tax (CGT) when you redeem: 15% CGT if you hold for under one year, 12.5% for 1–4 years, and 0% if you hold for more than four years. This makes long-term equity fund investing genuinely tax-efficient for patient investors.

  
The filing difference is material. Take a PKR 500,000 investment in a CDNS Special Savings Certificate at 11.6% — that generates PKR 58,000 in annual profit. A filer pays WHT of approximately PKR 8,700 (15%), while a non-filer pays approximately PKR 17,400 (30%). The filing difference alone is worth PKR 8,700 per year on this single investment. Filing your annual income tax return on FBR's IRIS portal takes around 30 minutes for a salaried individual — the arithmetic makes it an obvious priority.

  
## What Happens in a Rate Cut Cycle

  
This is the macro question that every Pakistani investor should be asking in 2026. The SBP policy rate currently sits at **11.5%** as of May 2026, having fallen dramatically from its peak of 22% in mid-2023. The SBP's current stance is "holding," but market consensus leans toward further gradual cuts as CPI inflation (currently 7.0%) continues to moderate toward the SBP's medium-term target band.

  
**CDNS certificates lock in your rate at the time of purchase.** If you buy a Special Savings Certificate today at 11.6% and the SBP cuts rates to 8% next year, your SSC continues earning 11.6% for the remaining tenure — the rate is fixed at purchase and cannot be reduced by subsequent monetary policy changes. This is the single biggest advantage of CDNS in a falling-rate environment. Investors who locked in long-duration Defence Savings Certificates at 20%+ rates in 2023 are still earning those rates today.

  
**Money market mutual funds reprice continuously.** Their effective yields track prevailing interbank rates very closely, and when the SBP cuts its benchmark rate, money market fund returns fall within 2–4 weeks as the fund's short-duration assets mature and are reinvested at the new lower rate. This is the biggest disadvantage of money market funds relative to CDNS when rates are falling.

  
**Equity funds historically benefit from rate cuts.** Lower rates reduce the discount rate applied to future corporate earnings, which generally pushes stock prices higher. Lower borrowing costs also stimulate economic activity and improve corporate profitability. The KSE-100 rose approximately 180% as SBP rates fell from 22% to 12% between mid-2023 and early 2025. If you believe rates will fall further, a strategic approach is to lock some savings into a 3-year CDNS SSC at today's rate, while gradually shifting money market fund allocation toward equity funds to capture the likely upside.

  
## The Verdict: You Don't Have to Choose

  
The most effective Pakistani investment portfolios use *both*. The comparison is a false choice framed by marketing material from both CDNS branches and AMC sales teams — each of which has a commercial incentive to convince you to use only their product.

  
A common allocation for a middle-income Pakistani investor might look like this:

  

    - **30–40% in National Savings** — anchor your portfolio with zero-risk government returns, locked in at today's relatively attractive rates before further SBP cuts

    - **40–50% in Mutual Funds** — split between an Islamic money market fund for near-term liquidity and an equity fund for long-term growth

    - **10–20% in PSX dividend stocks** — for additional income and long-term capital appreciation from Pakistan's listed companies

  

  
> **Practical tip:** If you are unsure where to start, open a **money market mutual fund** with Al Meezan or NBP Funds for the portion of your savings you might need within 1–2 years. For longer-term money (3+ years), consider a CDNS Special Savings Certificate or an equity mutual fund. ## A Real-World Portfolio Example

  
Abstract comparisons only go so far. Here is what a PKR 500,000 portfolio distributed across three instrument types would look like in practice, using current rates and recent historical returns as reference points.

  
The allocation: **PKR 200,000 (40%) in a CDNS Special Savings Certificate** at 11.6%; **PKR 200,000 (40%) in Al Meezan Mutual Fund** (Islamic equity, 1-year return 13.2%); and **PKR 100,000 (20%) in MCB Bank shares** at a dividend yield of 8.92%.

  

    - **CDNS SSC — PKR 200,000:** At 11.6% with semi-annual profit payments, annual gross profit is PKR 23,200. After 15% WHT for a tax filer, net annual income is approximately PKR 19,720. This rate is guaranteed for the full 3-year tenor regardless of SBP moves. Zero capital risk.

    - **Al Meezan Mutual Fund — PKR 200,000:** Based on the fund's 1-year return of 13.2%, estimated growth is approximately PKR 26,400. If held for more than one year, CGT is 12.5% on gains (and 0% after four years), leaving estimated net gain around PKR 23,100. Past performance does not guarantee future results — this figure is illustrative, not a promise.

    - **MCB Bank shares — PKR 100,000:** At an 8.92% dividend yield, annual dividend income is approximately PKR 8,920. After 15% WHT for a filer, net dividend income is approximately PKR 7,582 per year. Share prices can rise or fall independently of the dividend yield.

  

  
**Combined estimated annual income:** Approximately PKR 50,402 net of tax on PKR 500,000 invested — a blended effective yield of around 10.1%. Critically, the CDNS portion provides a guaranteed income floor regardless of market conditions, the mutual fund provides managed growth, and the shares provide direct equity participation. This three-part structure — one guaranteed source, one managed source, one direct source — is the framework that long-term Pakistani wealth builders consistently use.

  
## Impact of the SBP Policy Rate

  
The State Bank of Pakistan's policy rate directly affects both instrument types. When the rate is high (as it was at 22% in 2023–24), National Savings rates and money market fund returns both rise together. When the SBP cuts rates — as it has been doing since mid-2024 — both fall. The critical difference is that **National Savings certificates lock in your rate at the time of purchase**, while money market funds continuously adjust to prevailing rates.

  
In a falling-rate environment like today's, locking into a long-duration CDNS certificate at today's rate can be advantageous — you secure a higher rate before it drops further. ## Frequently Asked Questions

  
**Q: Can I invest in both National Savings and mutual funds at the same time?**

Yes — most balanced Pakistani portfolios combine both. National Savings anchors the fixed-income portion while mutual funds provide liquidity and growth potential. There is no rule preventing you from holding CDNS certificates alongside mutual fund units. Many investors keep 3–6 months of living expenses in a liquid money market mutual fund while simultaneously holding a longer-duration CDNS certificate for their savings. Each instrument complements the other's weaknesses: CDNS gives you the safety and rate-lock that mutual funds cannot offer, while mutual funds give you the liquidity and growth potential that CDNS cannot match.

  
**Q: What is the minimum investment for National Savings vs mutual funds?**

National Savings Special Savings Certificates start from PKR 500, while Regular Income Certificates require PKR 50,000. Mutual funds are more accessible — most AMCs accept SIPs from PKR 1,000 per month and lump sums from PKR 5,000. Al Meezan Mutual Fund allows investments from as little as PKR 1,000, making it possible for a first-time investor to start building a portfolio with very modest amounts. This low barrier to entry means there is genuinely no financial reason to delay starting — the most important step is simply beginning.

  
**Q: Are National Savings profits guaranteed even if the government changes?**

Yes. National Savings certificates are sovereign obligations of Pakistan — the rate you lock in at purchase is guaranteed regardless of government changes or SBP rate movements. Only a full sovereign default (which has never occurred in Pakistan) would put these at risk. CDNS operates under the Ministry of Finance and its obligations transfer seamlessly across every successive government. This is why CDNS instruments are used by institutional investors and individuals alike as the risk-free reference rate for the domestic market.

  
**Q: What happens to my mutual fund if the AMC closes down?**

Your money is protected. SECP regulations require all AMC assets to be held in a separate trust by an independent trustee (typically a bank). If an AMC closes, a new manager is appointed or assets are liquidated and returned to investors. Your capital cannot be mixed with the AMC's own funds. This trust structure is a fundamental feature of Pakistan's mutual fund regulatory framework under the Non-Banking Finance Companies Rules. The trustee — typically a commercial bank such as MCB or Standard Chartered — holds all fund assets in custody, completely separate from the AMC's own balance sheet.

  
**Q: Which is better for a 3-year investment horizon — National Savings or mutual funds?**

For a strict 3-year horizon, a Special Savings Certificate (3-year tenor) offers a guaranteed rate locked in at purchase. If you are comfortable with some volatility and want higher potential returns, an income mutual fund or balanced fund over 3 years has historically outperformed CDNS rates, but without the capital guarantee. A practical blended approach is to put 50–60% into an SSC for the guaranteed floor, and 40–50% into an equity or income fund for growth upside. This way you protect most of your principal while still participating in potential market gains over the three-year window.

  
**Q: How is the profit from National Savings and mutual funds taxed in Pakistan?**

Both instruments are generally subject to withholding tax on the profit or income they generate, and rates often differ for filers versus non-filers under FBR rules. National Savings profit is typically taxed at source by CDNS, while mutual fund dividends and capital gains are handled by the AMC. Because tax rates and slabs change with each Finance Act, confirm the current rates for your filer status on fbr.gov.pk before estimating your net return.

  
**Q: Can I withdraw early, and will I lose profit if I do?**

Most open-end mutual funds let you redeem units on any business day, with proceeds usually credited within a few working days. National Savings certificates can also be encashed before maturity, but early encashment often means you receive a lower profit rate than if you had held to the full tenor. Always check the specific encashment schedule for your certificate at the CDNS branch or on the official National Savings website before deciding.

  
**Q: Are there Shariah-compliant options for both National Savings and mutual funds?**

Yes, the mutual fund industry offers a range of Islamic income, money market, and equity funds — Al Meezan, for example, runs several Shariah-compliant funds screened by a Shariah board. National Savings has historically offered some Islamic-structured products as well, though availability can change over time. If Shariah compliance is essential to you, verify the current product list and the latest Shariah certification directly with the AMC or with CDNS.

  
**Q: Where do I actually buy National Savings certificates versus mutual funds?**

National Savings certificates are purchased at CDNS National Savings Centres, at authorised commercial bank branches, and increasingly through online channels offered by CDNS. Mutual funds are bought directly from an Asset Management Company or through its app and website, and sometimes via bank or brokerage distribution partners. Both processes require completing basic KYC documentation such as CNIC and bank details, so confirm the exact onboarding steps with the provider you choose.

  
**Q: Which option better protects my savings against inflation?**

Neither instrument guarantees a return above inflation, since real returns depend on how the profit rate compares with the inflation rate at the time. A fixed-rate National Savings certificate locks in a known nominal rate, which protects you if rates fall but can lag if inflation rises sharply. Equity and some income mutual funds carry more risk but have historically offered the potential for higher returns that may keep pace with inflation over longer periods — without any guarantee.
