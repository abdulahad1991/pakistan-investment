National Savings vs Mutual Funds: Which Is Better for Pakistani Investors? (2026)

By Abdul Ahad  ·  Updated May 2026  ·  9 min read

This is the most common question Pakistani investors ask: should I put my money in a National Savings certificate at CDNS, or invest it in a mutual fund through an AMC? Both are legitimate, regulated options — but they serve different needs, carry different risks, and suit different types of investors.

This guide gives you a clear, honest comparison so you can make the right choice for your specific situation.

National Savings — Strengths and Weaknesses

The Central Directorate of National Savings (CDNS) has been operating since 1947. Its instruments are backed by the full faith of the Government of Pakistan — meaning the only scenario in which you lose money is a complete sovereign default, which has never occurred. This makes CDNS schemes genuinely risk-free for practical purposes.

National Savings is best for:
  • Retirees who need predictable monthly income (Regular Income Certificates)
  • Senior citizens and widows (Bahbood Savings Certificates offer the highest rates)
  • Investors with no internet access or smartphone
  • Locking in high rates before SBP cuts further reduce yields
  • Anyone who cannot afford to lose any principal

Key weakness: Liquidity. If you encash a National Savings certificate before maturity, you lose a portion of the profit you've earned. For example, if you encash a Special Savings Certificate (3-year tenor) after 18 months, you receive a lower profit rate for the period. This makes CDNS unsuitable as an emergency fund or for money you might need at short notice.

Mutual Funds — Strengths and Weaknesses

Mutual funds offer two things National Savings cannot: higher potential returns and daily liquidity. A well-selected equity fund can deliver 20–30% annualised returns over a 5-year period, far exceeding any CDNS scheme. Even money market mutual funds — the safest mutual fund type — match or marginally exceed current National Savings rates while allowing you to withdraw your money within days.

Mutual Funds are best for:
  • Working professionals aged 25–50 who can tolerate short-term volatility
  • Investors with a 3–10 year time horizon who want to beat inflation
  • Muslim investors seeking fully Shariah-compliant investments (Islamic funds)
  • Salaried individuals wanting to automate monthly savings via SIPs
  • Overseas Pakistanis (via Roshan Digital Account)

Key weakness: No capital guarantee. An equity fund can lose 20–30% of its value in a bear market. Investors who panic and sell at the bottom lock in real losses. If you cannot psychologically or financially handle seeing your investment drop in value, start with a money market or income fund rather than an equity fund.

The Verdict: You Don't Have to Choose

The most effective Pakistani investment portfolios use both. A common allocation for a middle-income Pakistani investor might look like this:

  • 30–40% in National Savings — anchor your portfolio with zero-risk government returns
  • 40–50% in Mutual Funds — split between an Islamic money market fund and an equity fund for growth
  • 10–20% in PSX dividend stocks — for additional income and long-term capital appreciation
Practical tip: If you're unsure where to start, open a money market mutual fund with Al Meezan or NBP Funds for the portion of your savings you might need within 1–2 years. Impact of the SBP Policy Rate

The State Bank of Pakistan's policy rate directly affects both instrument types. When the rate is high (as it was at 22% in 2023–24), National Savings rates and money market fund returns both rise together. When the SBP cuts rates — as it has been doing since mid-2024 — both fall. The critical difference is that National Savings certificates lock in your rate at the time of purchase, while money market funds continuously adjust to prevailing rates.

In a falling-rate environment, locking into a long-duration CDNS certificate (such as a 10-year Defence Savings Certificate) at today's rate can be advantageous — you secure a higher rate before it drops further. Read our SBP Policy Rate guide for a deeper explanation.
